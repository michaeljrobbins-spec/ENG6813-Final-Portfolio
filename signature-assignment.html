Context for Delivery in the Course

This assignment (or perhaps more appropriately, this unit attached to a project) is intended to be used in Freshman Composition I (ENC1101) at any state university or community college. For this project's purposes, I will use my current institution (Valencia College). At Valencia College, faculty are required to use the three state learning outcomes designated for ENC1101:

  • Students will engage in writing processes that involve drafting, revising, and reflecting.
  • Students will apply rhetorical knowledge to communicate for a range of audiences and purposes.
  • Students will employ critical thinking to analyze forms of communication.

For this task, students will focus on the first and third outcomes (highlighted above); the goal of this project is to get students to collaborate and develop a draft policy based on collective classroom discussion. This will get them engaging in the writing process and thinking critically about sample writing or sources that can inform their values related to academic integrity.

Like many institutions, Valencia College runs either a full-term course (16 weeks) or a part-of-term course (8 weeks). For this Signature Assignment Project, I will be designing for a full-term course; future iterations of the project may attempt to develop for a part-of-term course, but for this initial project, it is simpler to showcase how to develop it with adequate time in the semester. The accelerated version of the course raises too many complications (e.g., ensuring students have adequate time to review data, addressing students' concerns throughout the process). In this full-term course, the project would be given after the midterm, roughly during weeks 9 through 11.

For course modality (at Valencia, this would be asynchronous online or synchronous face-to-face or hybrid mixed mode), this project will focus on courses that meet in the face-to-face onsite synchronous modality. An asynchronous version of the course — or even a synchronous hybrid mixed mode version — would require different facilitation from the faculty, including development of discussion board posts and new assessment methods (e.g., rubrics that evaluate those discussion posts). A synchronous, onsite face-to-face version of this assignment removes those additional assessments, since the material can be facilitated in face-to-face conversations. For the sake of this hypothetical, let's say the course meets every Monday and Wednesday from 10:00 AM until 11:15 AM. Future iterations may include sample discussion posts or online facilitation tools that could encourage student participation in an asynchronous online modality or a synchronous hybrid mixed modality.

In terms of where students will be at this point in the course, they have had the first half of the course to learn basics for rhetoric and composition; this includes elements of the writing process, introductions to rhetoric (including the classical rhetorical situation), early documentation and synthesis of source material (in my course, I teach both APA and MLA format), and critical analysis of an assignment prompt. This gives students a strong sense of some of the rhetorical terms that will be used in this project, such as understanding purpose in writing, audience, conventions of genre, etc. It also means the students will be familiar with reviewing an assignment prompt, reading a rubric, and understanding the basics of process-oriented writing skills (e.g., brainstorming, shaping or outlining the writing, drafting, revising, editing, and publishing).

This project was developed based on a draft version given in a previous discussion post in ENG6813 (“AI Exercise Design”, posted June 21, 2026). In this initial posting, students were drafting a revised version of an existing academic integrity policy. In this original design, students developed the project over three to five weeks. This wasn't intended to be a single day exercise, but to be a longer-term project students completed over several weeks in the course — a unit of study, essentially. This version of the project will be completed over three weeks; this allows students time to engage in the writing process, considering both their personal viewpoints and source samples that can inform the development of their draft policy. I also decided to remove the AI artifact, as it doesn't contribute to the learning outcomes for the current iteration of the project (this new iteration of the project focuses more on the drafting of a policy; the AI artifact would work better as part of a revision process).

I will be asking students to develop the project in three phases:

  • Phase 1 (Week 9): Collective Personal Values Inventory Statement
  • Phase 2 (Week 10): Collective Research Contribution
  • Phase 3 (Week 11): Class Academic Policy Statement and Personal Reflection

This is intended to be a collaborative project (both small group and full class), where students are focused on developing and evaluating the writing with a larger group. Class caps at Valencia are currently set to 27 for ENC1101; for the purposes of this project, we'll assume full enrollment and presume the small groups will have three to four students per group, meaning seven to nine groups total developing small group contributions. I'm keeping the group size smaller (three to four) to allow multiple student voices to emerge in the conversation, but large enough that absences won't harm the group dynamic too much; this is hopeful, as I've had experience with small group projects where multiple students are absent. For purposes of this project, let's presume an ideal full course with only two to three students absent each class session.

Additionally, there are touchpoints for personal development and reflection; these are primarily reflective components where students think about their contribution to the development of the policy (self evaluation check-ins). The final summative assessment will also primarily be individual reflection, with students thinking both about how their thoughts on academic integrity developed as a result of completing this draft and how their views about process-oriented writing grew.

I touched on this briefly, but one last note that needs to be considered for context in delivering the assignment: This project requires a lot of in-class engagement. However, there will likely be students who may have to miss class (emergencies happen). Given this reality of a classroom experience, alternative pathways will be given to allow for students who miss class to still engage in the course project in a meaningful way; these options will be noted at the end of each section, giving instructions for how faculty may engage students who miss class, including best practices for outreach to those students, versions of their engagement out-of-class, and means of assessing their overall progress toward course learning outcomes. The intention is not to punish a student for circumstances that may be out of their control. My own teaching philosophy is to try to design assessments with grace to the student's needs in mind. This means designing to where we don't punish a student for circumstances that might be outside of their control.

This alternative pathway does place a coordination burden on the group, since they must incorporate a peer's written input without that student present to clarify or negotiate in real time. I've kept it this way regardless, because the point is for an absent student's actual voice to become part of the group's real output, not a side assignment that never touches the shared document; a fully separate assignment for absent students would exclude them from the actual collaborative artifact the rest of the class produces.

Assignment Prompt 1 (Week 9): Collective Personal Values Inventory

Learning Outcomes for this Assignment

Students will engage in writing processes that involve drafting, revising, and reflecting.

Overview

This will be a collective assignment; you're all going to work on this as a class! Over the next three weeks, you will develop a draft policy for Valencia College. This will be your version of an academic integrity policy. This policy should be a reflection of our collective values, but it must also consider the standards of both academic and professional communities.

We'll complete this task in three phases: (1) Personal Values; (2) Academic Standards (Research); (3) Draft Class Academic Policy. We are currently in Phase 1: Personal Values.

For this first phase of the project, we are going to start with your group's personal values related to academic integrity. You're going to have a conversation with each other about what you think is most important for academic integrity in colleges and universities. This will require very little research on your part; I'm only going to ask you all to take a moment to review Valencia College's existing academic integrity policy.

Assignment Prompt 2 (Week 10): Collective Research Contribution

Learning Outcomes for this Assignment

Students will employ critical thinking to analyze forms of communication.

Overview

This is a continuation of what you all worked on in Week 9. Remember that over the next three weeks, you will develop a draft policy for Valencia College. For this week, you must start to consider the standards of both academic and professional communities.

Remember: We'll complete this task in three phases: (1) Personal Values; (2) Academic Standards (Research); (3) Draft Class Academic Policy. We are currently in Phase 2: Academic Standards. To learn more about these academic standards, you'll have to conduct research!

For this phase, each of you will individually identify one source. Then, in your small group, you'll debate which two of your individually-found sources your group will collectively use — including negotiating why a given source should (or shouldn't) make the final cut. This means not every source you personally find will end up in your group's final submission, and that's expected: the debate itself is part of the assignment.

Here is a list of the type of sources you are looking for that will help us in our drafting process.

  1. Sample Academic Integrity Policies: Identify a sample academic integrity policy that has been published by a university or college in the United States. This can be any institution except for Valencia College.
  2. Peer Reviewed Academic Research: Find a sample peer reviewed journal article that focuses on academic integrity. This might include research that demonstrates current trends in cheating, qualitative studies of student views on academic integrity, or faculty concerns related to academic integrity. For our purposes, current means that the article needs to have been published post-2020 (after the Pandemic).
  3. Popular Media from Reputable Authors: This can be editorials or feature articles published by a reputable organization, such as a news magazine or a trade publication related to higher education. The article may be written by a professional in the field (a professor or administrator at a college), a journalist interviewing professionals in the field, or a student currently attending an institution. It must provide insight related to academic integrity.

These sources can be in multiple media formats (written, audio, visual, etc.). Individually, you may bring any of the three source types to the group debate; but the group's final two selections may use only two of those three types, with no overlap between the source types. For example:

Example source-type selections

Group 1 (Unsuccessful)Selects two published academic integrity policies (Harvard and the University of Florida). This is unsuccessful because both selections are the same source type.
Group 2 (Successful)Selects an editorial published in Inside Higher Ed and the University of Florida's academic integrity policy. This is successful because the two selections represent varied source types.

Assignment Prompt 1 (Week 9): Collective Personal Values Inventory

For this week's assignment, you all will be providing a statement of your Collective Personal Values. A successful version of this assignment should answer the four guiding questions listed in the prompt above. I asked an LLM tool (Claude) to develop a sample for you all to evaluate. Here is the sample it developed:

Collective Personal Values Inventory Statement — Group 4 (Maria T., Devon R., Priya S., Jake M.)

To us, academic integrity means doing our own thinking, even when it's easier not to. It's not just “don't cheat on tests” — it's being honest about what's actually ours versus what came from somewhere else, whether that's a classmate, a textbook, or an AI tool. We think integrity is really about trust: our professors trust that our work reflects our own effort, and that trust is what makes grades and degrees mean something. Our ideas mostly came from personal experience rather than any formal source. A few of us had classes in high school where AI use was either completely banned or a total free-for-all, and neither approach felt fair or realistic. We also talked about times we've seen classmates cut corners under pressure, which shaped how we think about the “why” behind cheating, not just the “what.” Comparing our values to Valencia's existing policy, we think we align on the basics (plagiarism and dishonesty are wrong), but we noticed the current policy doesn't say much about AI specifically beyond a general mention. That's the biggest gap between what we believe and what's written down. We think our values should be included because we're the ones actually living with this policy day to day. A policy written only by administrators can miss what students are really facing right now, especially with how fast AI tools are changing.

Notice the sample used a collective personal voice (e.g., “we” or “us”). Notice it also shared individual examples where possible; that's the specific concrete examples, like when it talked about the experiences in high school. Your sample can use a similar structure to this, addressing it part-by-part. Please note that you all need to develop your own original response; an AI tool can be used to help in your brainstorming process, but your collective contribution should be an original answer.

Assignment Prompt 2 (Week 10): Collective Research Contribution

For this week's assignment, your group will be providing two sources with annotations — the two your group selected after debating your individually-found candidates. You can break your answer into these parts:

Part I: Bibliographic Entry. This is an MLA or APA formatted bibliographic entry that you provide for the source. Note that you do have to be consistent in your formatting (i.e., both entries must use either MLA or APA format; you can't use MLA for one and APA for the other).

Part II: Annotation. Your annotation should include a brief summary of the source, why it is relevant to our discussion of academic integrity, how it influenced your group's thinking, and why it won out over another candidate your group considered. Each annotation should be 100 to 200 words.

I asked an LLM tool (Claude) to develop a sample for you all to evaluate. Here is the sample it developed:

Collective Research Contribution Entry 1

Sbaffi, Laura, and Xin Zhao. “Evaluating a Pedagogical Approach to Promoting Academic Integrity in Higher Education: An Online Induction Program.” Frontiers in Psychology, vol. 13, 2022, article 1009305, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1009305.

This peer-reviewed study looked at how a UK university redesigned its academic integrity resources for incoming graduate students after realizing students found the original materials confusing and overly harsh in tone. The researchers found that students wanted real examples and relatable scenarios rather than dense definitions, and they also wanted the tone of academic integrity messaging to feel supportive rather than punitive or “scary.” After redesigning the resources with interactive quizzes and clearer examples, student confidence in understanding academic integrity concepts nearly doubled. This source is relevant to our policy because it's not really about catching cheaters — it's about how a policy gets communicated actually changes whether students take it seriously. It made our group rethink our own draft: we'd been focused only on what counts as a violation, but this source pushed us to also think about tone. It won out over a classmate's proposed institutional-policy source because we already had a policy-type source and needed a research source to avoid overlap.

Notice this sample used a more impersonal voice — annotations tend to be written in a more objective voice, though when it got to relevancy, it became a bit more personal. Also note the sentence about why the source won out over another candidate — this is where your group's debate becomes visible in the final annotation. The key to a good annotation is talking about the source's conclusions, the most important details, and why it made the cut.

Assignment Prompt 3 (Week 11): Class Academic Integrity Policy (and your Personal Reflection)

For this week's assignment, you will be developing a 600 to 800 word personal reflection on the drafting of our class academic integrity policy, addressing the four questions listed in the prompt above.

I would recommend that you address each bullet as a paragraph; remember that a successful paragraph includes the following parts:

Identification of a Topic: This is where you'll state what the goal of the paragraph is. You should connect this to the bullet provided.

Presentation of Example or Evidence: This is where you'll provide evidence or examples that show your topic is tenable (that is, it can be proven through evidence).

Relevancy to Thesis: This is where you discuss how your topic relates to the thesis; you'll want to explain how what you're talking about in this paragraph connects to the overall goals of the piece of writing.

Transitions to New Paragraph (Optional): As you close or open your paragraph, you'll want to connect ideas — that is, you might provide a transitional thought or statement that leads logically to the next point you intend to make.

Here is a color-coded sample paragraph developed by an LLM tool (Claude):

Sample Paragraph (Contributions)

One place I see my contribution most clearly is in the policy's final line about AI disclosure, which states that students must explain not just whether they used an AI tool, but why they chose to use it. That specific framing came directly from something I said during our Week 11 discussion: I argued that simply checking a box for “AI used: yes/no” didn't capture the more complicated reality of how AI actually gets used in the writing process. When my group first talked about this in Week 9, we'd focused mostly on values like honesty and trust, but I didn't think those values addressed the murkier, in-between moments of AI use, like using it to brainstorm but not to write. I pushed for language that asked students to reflect on purpose, not just presence. Seeing that idea show up nearly word-for-word in our final class policy made me realize how much a single voice in a large group discussion can shape a shared document, especially when that voice pushes back on an assumption everyone else has accepted. This also reminded me that writing collaboratively doesn't mean losing your individual perspective; it means finding where your perspective actually needs to be heard. That realization carries into how I think about the next question, about whether this policy matches my own personal philosophy.

Notice that the sample focuses on the evidence — this tends to be the more significant part of a paragraph, and it's where you'll give the clear, concrete details that your audience needs to understand your topic. It's what takes your writing from an unclear, uncertain abstraction to a specific, concrete example that the audience can connect to. Don't forget that we are using MLA or APA format for this paper! You can refer to our Canvas resources, or you can go to the Purdue OWL for additional support (links available in Canvas).

Rubrics or Criteria for Grading

For each stage of the assignment where there is a meaningful formative or summative assessment, students will be evaluated with a rubric. I use a modified version of a specification rubric in all of my courses; students are evaluated on one of the following standards: Full Credit (Meets criterion), Half Credit (Partially meets criterion), or No Credit (Does not meet criterion). In each case, more specific details are provided that explain the reasoning for the selection. Specific feedback will also be provided using the Canvas annotation and rubric tools.

Weighting: Specific numeric point values have not been attached to these rubrics, as that would require additional contextualization into the point-value system used in the course. However, each criterion below includes a percentage weight of that rubric's total, so word count and formatting don't read as equal in importance to the substance of the response — substance-related criteria (answering the questions, specific detail, annotation quality) are weighted higher than mechanical criteria like word count and formatting.

Revision policy: Students who land in the half-credit or no-credit range on the weekly formative checkpoints (Rubrics 1 through 4, covering the Week 9 and Week 10 group and self-evaluation submissions) receive written feedback but are not guaranteed a resubmission window — they're asked to apply that feedback going forward in the project instead. The guaranteed two-day revision-and-resubmit window is reserved for the Week 11 Personal Reflection (Rubric 5), the project's one individually graded summative piece. This keeps revision support concentrated where it matters most for the final grade, rather than adding a resubmission cycle to every weekly checkpoint.

In deference to these considerations, students will be given optional guidance to use AI tools as part of brainstorming or to assist in research processes.

AI Usage in this Project

For this project, Claude was used as part of my ideation process, as well as to prepare samples for the students and develop rubrics. It was used in the following specific ways:

On showing students that AI can complete this kind of assignment: Using AI-generated samples raises a real tension I want to name directly: showing students a competent AI-written values statement or annotation risks signaling that the assignment itself is something AI can simply do for them, which cuts against the point of a collaborative, process-focused project.
